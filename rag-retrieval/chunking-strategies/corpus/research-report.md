# Predicting late deliveries on cross-border lanes

Tidewater Freight, Data Science team. Internal research report DS-2026-07. Authors: Amara Okafor, Lucas Ferreira, Ines Vidal, Tomasz Wrona.

## Abstract

Late deliveries on cross-border lanes cost Tidewater Freight more in service credits than any other lane category, yet our existing estimated-time-of-arrival service treats a border crossing as a fixed delay added to the road segment. This report describes a classifier that predicts, at the moment a shipment is tendered, whether it will arrive more than four hours after its committed delivery window. We trained gradient-boosted trees on eighteen months of tender, telematics and customs-filing records covering 412,000 shipments across 37 lanes between the United States, Canada and Mexico. On held-out data the model separates late from on-time shipments substantially better than the current rule-based flag, and its most useful signal is not distance or carrier but the completeness of the customs paperwork at tender time. We discuss what the model can and cannot be used for, and recommend a limited pilot on the Laredo and Detroit corridors before any customer-facing use.

## 1 Introduction

A shipment on a cross-border lane passes through at least three handoffs that a domestic shipment never sees: the export filing, the physical crossing, and the import release. Each handoff has its own queue, its own working hours and its own failure modes, and delays at one handoff tend to compound at the next because a truck that misses the afternoon release window at a port of entry often waits until the following morning. Our current late-delivery flag, which the operations team calls the red-lane rule, marks a shipment as at risk when its planned transit time exceeds the lane's historical median by more than twenty percent. The rule was introduced in 2022 and was a reasonable first step, but it only looks at the plan, not at the condition of the shipment itself. In practice the operations team told us that most of the shipments they rescue are ones the rule never flagged, and most of the shipments the rule flags arrive on time anyway. That observation is the starting point for this work.

The commercial stakes are concrete. Under the standard service level terms in our customer agreements, a delivery more than four hours outside the committed window triggers a service credit, and cross-border lanes produced 61 percent of all service credits paid in the 2025 fiscal year while carrying only 23 percent of shipment volume. A prediction made at tender time gives the operations team roughly a day and a half to intervene, which is enough to chase missing documents, rebook a crossing appointment, or warn the customer before the window is missed rather than after. We therefore framed the problem as binary classification at a single decision point, tender, rather than as continuous arrival-time regression, because the interventions available to the team are discrete and are only cheap early.

## 2 Related work

Most published work on arrival-time prediction in freight focuses on domestic long-haul trucking, where the dominant sources of variance are traffic, weather and driver hours-of-service limits. Those factors matter on our lanes too, but they are not where cross-border lanes differ from domestic ones. Internally, the routing team's earlier study of the Nogales corridor found that customs dwell time had a heavier right tail than any road segment on the same lane, with the slowest five percent of crossings taking longer than the entire remaining road journey [Okafor 2025, internal]. A second internal study tried to predict dwell time directly from the port of entry and the hour of arrival, and concluded that those two variables explained little of the variance once the paperwork status was known [Wrona 2025, internal]. That conclusion shaped our feature design more than any external reference did. We also borrowed the evaluation discipline of the pricing team's demand forecasts, which always report results on a later time period than the training data rather than on a random split, because random splits leak seasonal patterns and flatter the model [Ferreira 2024, internal].

## 3 Data

### 3.1 Sources

We joined four sources at the shipment level. Tender records from the order system provide the lane, the customer, the committed delivery window, the declared commodity class and the tender timestamp. Telematics pings from carrier devices provide the actual pickup, border arrival, border release and delivery times, which we used only to build the label and never as features, since none of them exist at tender time. Customs-filing records from our brokerage integration provide, for each shipment, which of the required documents had been submitted at the moment of tender and which were still pending. Finally, the carrier scorecard provides each carrier's on-time rate over the trailing ninety days, computed as of the tender date so that it never includes the outcome of the shipment being scored.

### 3.2 Splits and labels

The dataset covers tenders from January 2024 through June 2025, a total of 412,000 shipments after removing cancelled tenders and shipments with no delivery ping. A shipment is labelled late when its delivered timestamp falls more than four hours after the end of its committed delivery window, which matches the contractual definition that triggers a service credit; 14.2 percent of shipments meet this definition. We trained on tenders from January 2024 through March 2025, used April and May 2025 for early stopping and threshold selection, and held out every tender from June 2025 as the test set. Because June is the start of the produce season on the southern lanes, the held-out month is harder than an average month, and we chose it deliberately rather than averaging over a random sample. All results in Section 5 are on this held-out June 2025 data unless a sentence says otherwise.

### 3.3 Known gaps

Telematics coverage is incomplete for a minority of small carriers who report status by email rather than through a device. For those shipments we reconstructed delivery times from proof-of-delivery scans, which have a typical delay of under an hour, and we kept them in the dataset because dropping them would have removed most of the small-carrier volume. Customs-filing records before March 2024 lack the timestamp of each individual document submission, so for the first two months we only know whether the filing was complete at the end of the day rather than at the moment of tender.

## 4 Method

The model is a gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble with 600 trees of depth six, trained with a learning rate of 0.05 and early stopping on the April–May validation window. We chose boosted trees over a neural sequence model because almost all of our features are tabular facts known at a single moment, because the operations team needs per-shipment explanations, and because a tree ensemble trains in minutes on a single machine, which made it practical to retrain weekly. The feature set has 41 inputs grouped into five families: lane and route, customer and commodity, timing, carrier history, and documentation. The documentation family is new to this work and consists of a count of pending required documents, a flag for whether the commercial invoice has been submitted, a flag for whether the commodity requires a permit from a partner agency, and the number of hours between tender and the earliest available crossing appointment.

We compared three systems. The first is the red-lane rule described in the introduction, scored as a binary flag. The second is a logistic regression on the same 41 features, which tells us how much of the gain comes from the features rather than from the model class. The third is the gradient-boosted model. For the boosted model we set the decision threshold on the validation window so that the operations team would review at most 900 shipments per day across all cross-border lanes, which is their stated capacity, and we report precision and recall at that operating point alongside threshold-free measures. Explanations are produced with per-shipment feature attributions and are shown to reviewers as the three features that pushed the score up the most.

## 5 Results

Table 2 summarises the comparison on the held-out set. AUC is the area under the ROC curve; precision and recall are measured at the operating threshold described in Section 4.

| System | AUC | Precision at threshold | Recall at threshold | Shipments flagged per day |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Red-lane rule | 0.63 | 0.21 | 0.38 | 1,240 |
| Logistic regression | 0.74 | 0.33 | 0.49 | 900 |
| Gradient-boosted trees | 0.81 | 0.44 | 0.58 | 900 |

The boosted model's AUC of 0.81 is a clear improvement over the red-lane rule's 0.63, and the gap between the logistic regression and the boosted model suggests that interactions between features matter, for example between the commodity class and the permit flag. At the same review capacity of 900 shipments per day, the boosted model flags shipments of which 44 percent are actually late, against 21 percent for the rule, so a reviewer spends roughly half as much time on shipments that would have been fine. Recall at the threshold is 0.58, meaning the model still misses about two in five late shipments; most of the misses are shipments that left on time with complete paperwork and were delayed by events after tender, such as a port-of-entry closure or a mechanical breakdown, which no tender-time model can foresee.

The feature attributions tell a consistent story. Across all flagged shipments in the test month, the count of pending required documents was the largest single contributor to the score, ahead of lane, carrier on-time rate and commodity class. A shipment tendered with two or more required documents still pending was late 39 percent of the time, compared with 9 percent for shipments tendered with complete paperwork. The hours between tender and the earliest crossing appointment was the second most important feature, and its effect was sharply non-linear: below eighteen hours the late rate rose steeply, while above thirty-six hours it made almost no difference. Carrier history mattered less than the operations team expected, which we discuss below.

We also measured stability across lanes. On the eleven lanes with at least 5,000 test-period shipments, the model's AUC ranged from 0.76 on the Nogales corridor to 0.86 on the Detroit–Windsor corridor. The Nogales result is the lowest because produce shipments dominate that lane in June and their delays are driven by agricultural inspections that our data does not describe in any detail.

## 6 Discussion

The strongest finding is that documentation status at tender time predicts lateness better than anything about the road. This agrees with the earlier dwell-time study and gives the operations team an intervention that is entirely in their control: chasing a missing commercial invoice is a phone call, while a slow road is not something anyone can fix. It also explains why the red-lane rule performed poorly, because the rule only sees the planned transit time and is blind to the condition of the paperwork, which is the main reason a shipment that looks normal on the plan ends up late. We think the useful product is therefore not a late-delivery score in isolation, but a score paired with the specific documents that are missing.

The modest importance of carrier history surprised the operations team, who rely heavily on carrier scorecards when assigning loads. One plausible reading is that dispatchers already avoid poor carriers on cross-border lanes, so the carriers that appear in the data are mostly good ones and the remaining variation between them is small. Under that reading, carrier history is not unimportant in general; it has simply been used up by an existing human process. If the model were used to make dispatch decisions directly, this would change, and the carrier features would need to be re-evaluated on data that the model itself had not shaped. We flag this as a feedback-loop risk for any future deployment that lets the score influence carrier selection.

## 7 Limitations

The test period is a single month, and although we chose a hard month on purpose, one month cannot show whether performance holds through the winter season, when weather closures dominate the northern lanes. The label inherits the contractual four-hour definition, so a shipment that arrives three hours late is treated exactly like one that arrives on time, which is right for service credits but may not match what customers actually care about. The model has not been evaluated on lanes that were added after June 2025, including the new Calgary–Monterrey route, and we would not expect it to transfer to a lane whose crossing it has never seen. Finally, because the documentation features depend on the brokerage integration, shipments brokered by a third party have those features missing, and the model's precision on that subset fell to 0.29 in the test month.

## 8 Conclusion

A tender-time classifier built mostly on documentation and appointment timing identifies late cross-border shipments substantially better than the planned-transit rule it would replace, at the same review workload. We recommend a twelve-week pilot on the Laredo and Detroit corridors in which reviewers see the score together with the list of pending documents, with the red-lane rule kept running in parallel so that the two can be compared on the same shipments. Customer-facing use, such as proactive late notices, should wait until the pilot has measured how often an intervention actually prevents a late delivery, because a prediction that is never acted upon has no value to the customer and some risk of eroding their trust.
