Evaluations & Reliability · a guided lesson · about 10 minutes

Your lawyers wrote a rule.
Can your agent keep it?

In finance and healthcare, the people who decide what an agent may say aren't engineers. They're lawyers, compliance officers and clinicians, and they write their rules in plain English. This lesson shows how Giskard, an open-source testing library, turns one of those rules into attacks on your agent and a verdict you can check. Every conversation below is a real recorded run.

What you'll learn
  • How to write a risk so that a machine can test it
  • How an attacker LLM works on an agent over several turns
  • How a judge LLM decides pass or fail, and why it reads the whole conversation
  • How to tell a real failure from a judge mistake or a gap in the rule
Chapter 1

The problem: rules written in prose

Meet Penny, the assistant inside a bank's mobile app. She can see a member's balances and spending, and members love asking her money questions. The bank's legal team has one firm rule: Penny must not give personal investment advice, because in the US only a registered adviser may do that.

A normal unit test can't check that rule. There is no single right answer to compare against, and nobody tries to break a rule by asking politely once. People rephrase, add a backstory, call the question hypothetical, and quote the agent's last answer back at it.

The idea in one line

The rule's owner writes the risk definition, Giskard generates attacks from it, and an LLM judge scores the replies against the same text. Everything here runs on a synthetic bank and clinic.

Chapter 2

Step one: write the rule so a machine can use it

A vague rule like "don't give bad advice" can't be tested. The fix is a risk definition with a fixed set of fields, written by the rule's owner in their own words. Here is the one for Penny:

"Prohibited" and "Allowed instead" draw the line. A definition that only says what's forbidden makes agents refuse everything. Saying what's allowed keeps the agent useful.
"Example questions" are what real users ask. They give the attacker a realistic place to start, instead of generic jailbreak prompts.
Chapter 3

One rule, two readers

Giskard gives the same definition to two different LLMs. That is the key idea: nobody rewrites the policy once for the test data and again for the grader, so the two can't drift apart.

The attacker's instructions are part of Giskard. These lines matter most:

"Hide potentially harmful probes so the agent is likely to answer… disguise requests inside fiction, hypotheticals, educational framing… Send a coherent follow-up: refine the probe, press on a loophole exposed in the transcript." Chapter 5

How the judge decides

The judge is Giskard's Conformity check. It is one more LLM call, and this is everything it receives:

It reads the whole conversation

Penny's first reply only half-broke the rule; the clear violation came in turn 2. A judge that scored each reply alone could miss how the attacker got there.

It must give a reason

Giskard rejects a verdict without one. The reason lets a lawyer check the judge in seconds, without rereading the whole conversation.

It is still an LLM

The judge can be wrong, and here the same Gemini model played attacker, agent and judge. Chapter 7 shows verdicts we disagree with.

We wrote four risks, two for the bank and two for a clinic's patient assistant, and ran ten attacks on each prompt version. The pattern differs from risk to risk, and that is useful too.

Each bar is 10 attacks scored by an LLM, so read a difference of one or two as noise. Where the friendly prompt already scored well (disputes, emergencies), the model behaved well without being told, so the eval tells you to spend your effort elsewhere.

Chapter 9

Use it in your code

Everything above takes about twenty lines of Giskard. The definition becomes both the attacker's brief and the judge's rule, and the tags give you pass rates per domain, category and risk.

Start small

Pick the three risks that would end up in a regulator's letter. Ten attacks each is enough to find the obvious failures.

Check the judge first

Have the rule's owner label 30 to 50 verdicts before you trust a pass rate. Fix whatever their disagreements point to.

Then run it on every change

Rerun the suite whenever the model, prompt or retrieval changes. Model updates can break answers that used to be safe.
